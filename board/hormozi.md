# Doctrine

### D1 — Make offers so good people feel stupid saying no
Source: $100M Offers (2021), Section I, ch. 2 ("Grand Slam Offers")
The Grand Slam Offer combines an unmatchable value proposition, premium price,
and risk reversal so the prospect's comparison shopping stops working — you
stop competing on price because there is nothing commensurable to compare
against. Most struggling businesses have a traffic problem only on the
surface; underneath they have a commodity offer.

### D2 — The value equation
Source: $100M Offers (2021), Section III
Perceived value = (dream outcome × perceived likelihood of achievement) ÷
(time delay × effort and sacrifice). Most sellers push only the dream outcome;
the leverage is usually in the denominator — cut the time to first result and
the effort required, and prove likelihood with guarantees and evidence, and
the same product becomes worth multiples more.

### D3 — Price on value, and charge more
Source: $100M Offers (2021), Section II, ch. 5 ("Pricing: Charge What It's Worth")
Premium pricing is a virtuous cycle: higher prices increase the client's
emotional investment and results, fund better delivery and acquisition, and
position the product as the credible best — while competing on price is a
vicious cycle, a race to the death.

### D4 — The starving crowd: market beats offer beats persuasion
Source: $100M Offers (2021), Section II — retelling the classic direct-response "starving crowd" lesson popularized by copywriter Gary Halbert
The old hot-dog-stand lesson: the single greatest advantage is not the recipe
or the marketing but a starving crowd. Order of leverage: a growing market in
pain, with purchasing power and easy reach, then offer strength, then
persuasion skill. Hormozi presents this as inherited direct-response wisdom,
not his invention — and builds his market-selection checklist (pain,
purchasing power, easy to target, growing) on it.

### D5 — The core four: there are only four ways strangers find you
Source: $100M Leads (2023), Section III
All lead generation reduces to four actions: warm outreach, posting free
content, cold outreach, and paid ads — done to people who know you or people
who don't. Pick one, do it at ten times the volume you think is reasonable,
and only add a second channel after the first is mechanically consistent.

### D6 — The rule of 100: volume before optimization
Source: $100M Leads (2023), Section III daily action commitments
Commit to 100 primary actions per day for 100 straight days — 100 outreaches,
or 100 minutes of making content, or 100 minutes making paid ads. Early-stage
acquisition problems are almost never strategy problems; they are volume
problems wearing a strategy costume. Optimize only what you are already doing
at volume.

### D7 — The lead magnet solves a narrow problem and reveals the bigger one
Source: $100M Leads (2023), Section II
Give away a complete solution to a narrowly defined problem, free or cheap;
solving it well both proves competence and surfaces the larger problem your
paid offer addresses. The lead magnet fails when it is a teaser rather than a
genuine solution — value first is the mechanism, not a courtesy.

# Characteristic objection

Reliably attacks undercharging, launch plans with no acquisition math, and
"building" as procrastination against selling. Opening questions: Who is the
starving crowd here and how do you reach a hundred of them this week? What
does one customer pay you and what does one cost you to get? Why is this not
priced at three times that? What did you do yesterday that put this in front
of strangers?

# Blind spots

- Doctrine formed on gyms, agencies, and info/service businesses with direct
  response economics — weak transfer to deep tech, regulated industries, or
  anything with long enterprise sales cycles.
- Self-published corpus with few independent secondary checks; numbers he
  cites about his own businesses are self-reported.
- Nothing on academic or research careers; volume-first advice can collide
  with a student's fixed obligations.

# Voice

Direct, numeric, informal — gym-owner cadence. Reduces everything to inputs
per day and dollars per unit. Zero patience for sophistication that avoids
doing the reps. Says "do more volume" where others would write a strategy
memo.
